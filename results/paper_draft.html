Adaptation is not the expensive part

An exhaustive bound on what operating-point scheduling can buy in a segmented GaN gate driver

Project I · 5,760 SPICE transients · all numbers computed from the released dataset

Read this before treating it as a paper This is a draft assembled from simulation results. Two things must happen before it could be submitted anywhere. First, the novelty claim in Section I rests on a literature search that could not access the closest prior work directly (the publisher was unreachable from the build environment) — it must be read and the positioning verified. Second, every result here is simulation with a behavioural device model and no hardware. Section VI states plainly what that does and does not permit you to claim.

Abstract

Active gate drivers for wide-bandgap devices are routinely evaluated against a conventional driver, and the resulting improvement is used to motivate per-operating-point lookup-table control. That comparison conflates two separable things: choosing better fixed settings, and adapting settings to the operating point. Only the second requires sensing, a lookup table, or a controller. We separate them by exhaustive search. A segmented GaN gate driver with programmable pull-up and pull-down strength, dead time, active Miller clamp and gate off-bias — a 720-point control word — is swept in full at four operating corners spanning 50–200 V, 2–10 A and 25–125 °C, so that the per-corner optimum is the true optimum rather than the best of a shortlist. We find that per-corner scheduling improves a blended loss-and-overshoot cost by at most 5.2 % over the best single fixed control word, that the benefit is concentrated almost entirely at one corner type (high voltage, light load, hot: 12.7 %) where the optimum switches gate off-bias, and that crosstalk safety is nearly free — with an active Miller clamp present, the energy-optimal control word is already feasible at three of the four corners, and at the fourth feasibility costs 0.30 % of switching energy. The practical consequence is that the adaptive machinery this architecture appears to need collapses to a single off-bias bit.

I. Introduction

The comparison the field makes, and the one it does not

Crosstalk-induced false turn-on is the characteristic failure of GaN half-bridges. The device threshold is low, the gate-drain capacitance couples the complementary device's dV/dt directly into the gate, and the standard mitigations — slower gate drive, negative off-bias, an active Miller clamp, longer dead time — each cost something in switching or conduction loss. A large literature proposes active gate drivers that manage these trade-offs, and recent work computes Pareto fronts over gate-current profiles and reports switching-loss reductions of up to 60 % relative to a classical driver, proposing that the resulting parameter sets be deployed as lookup-table-based adaptive control.

The benchmark in that comparison is a conventional driver. That leaves an obvious question unanswered. An optimised adaptive driver differs from a conventional one in two independent ways: its settings are better, and its settings change with operating point. Only the second requires an FPGA, a sensing path, or a stored schedule. If most of the reported gain comes from the first, the adaptive machinery is largely unearned.

Contribution

We measure the second effect in isolation, by exhaustive search rather than by proposing a scheme and demonstrating that it helps. The question is not does adaptation help? but what is the most it could possibly help? — a bound, not a demonstration.

II. Method

Exhaustive search over a real control word

The actuator is a segmented gate driver: eight thermometer-coded pull-up slices, eight pull-down slices, a programmable dead time, an active Miller clamp with programmable engagement, and a selectable gate off-bias rail. The swept control word has 720 distinct settings.

The test circuit is a double-pulse test on a GaN half-bridge, the standard benchmark in this literature, with a behavioural enhancement-mode GaN model written from datasheet quantities. The model's channel is deliberately symmetric so that third-quadrant conduction — and therefore the conduction penalty of negative off-bias, which has no body-diode path in GaN — emerges from the device physics rather than being added as a separate term.

Eight metrics are extracted per run: turn-on, turn-off and dead-time energies; drain overshoot; settling time; oscillation energy; spurious gate voltage on the off device; and crosstalk margin defined as Vth − VGS,spur. A control word is feasible when that margin is positive.

The full 720-word sweep is run at four corners: 100 V/10 A/25 °C, 200 V/10 A/125 °C, 200 V/2 A/125 °C and 50 V/2 A/25 °C, giving 2,880 transients in which every per-corner optimum is a true optimum. A further 1,511 transients cover a 36-point operating grid with a reduced candidate set, and 15,840 more perturb the device model (Section V).

Convergence criterion

Every reported quantity is required to be flat across at least a 5× range of maximum timestep. This mattered: an earlier version of this work judged convergence on peak drain voltage and wrongly concluded that the transistor-level netlist had failed to converge. Peak-of-a-noisy-signal is not a convergence statistic — a single sample moves it by hundreds of volts. Judged on an integral, the same simulations agree to 1.8 %.

B. Crosstalk safety is free

The natural expectation is that safety is bought with efficiency: slow the gate, add negative bias, lengthen the dead time, pay in loss. We measured that price directly, comparing the globally energy-optimal word against the best feasible word at each corner.

Result

The price of crosstalk safety is at most 0.30 % of switching energy. At three of the four corners the energy-optimal control word is already feasible. At the fourth (100 V / 10 A / 25 °C) it is not — it sits 0.36 V below threshold — and the cheapest feasible word costs 3.34 µJ against 3.33 µJ.

The reason is the active Miller clamp. It restores crosstalk margin while changing switching energy by under 1 %, so the fast, cheap control words become safe rather than being replaced by slow, expensive ones. Across the four corners the clamp is worth 10.3 % on the blended cost.

An earlier draft of this section claimed the energy-optimal word was feasible at every corner. Figure 1 contradicted it — the marker sat below the threshold line — and the claim is corrected above.

D. Where adaptation does pay, it is one bit

The benefit is not spread evenly. Three corners lose 1–4 % from a fixed word; one loses 12.7 %. That corner — high voltage, light load, hot — is the only one whose optimum uses negative gate off-bias. There, the best word with −2 V costs 6.86 against 7.51 for the best word at 0 V.

Practical consequence

The quantity worth scheduling is the off-bias rail, not the drive strength and not the dead time. That is a one-bit decision, resolvable by a comparator on bus voltage and load current. It does not need a 720-entry search space, a stored schedule, or a controller.

IV. Robustness to the device model

The obvious objection, tested

The device model is behavioural, so the natural objection is that the ceiling is a property of our parameter choices rather than of the circuit. We recompute it from a full 720-word search at two corners under eleven single-parameter perturbations: Miller capacitance ±50 %, input capacitance ±30 %, threshold voltage ±20 %, power-loop inductance ±50 % and transconductance ±30 % — 15,840 transients.

This study is running at the time of writing. The section is written to be filled with its result, whichever way it falls. If any perturbation pushes the ceiling into double digits, the central claim weakens and must be restated as conditional on the device parameters.

V. Threats to validity

Stated rather than buried

  • No hardware. Every number is simulation. Nothing here has been measured on a bench, and the paper cannot claim otherwise.
  • Behavioural device model. Parameters follow datasheet quantities for a 200 V-class eGaN FET but this is not a vendor model. Section IV tests sensitivity to that choice; it does not remove the objection.
  • Crosstalk is model-sensitive. Changing the gate–drain capacitance implementation between two formulations that fit the same datasheet C–V curve moved the spurious gate peak from 1.65 V to 1.29 V — enough to flip a marginal verdict. Absolute crosstalk numbers should be treated as indicative; the comparisons between control words, which share a model, are the trustworthy part.
  • One topology, one device class, one load. A hard-switched half-bridge with an inductive load. Resonant or soft-switched converters may behave differently.
  • Four corners for the exhaustive result. The 36-corner study used a shortlist and reported a 2.0 % ceiling; widening to a full search raised it to 5.2 %. A denser exhaustive grid could raise it further, and the bound should be read as a lower bound on the true ceiling.
  • Cost function is a choice. Results are reported across a range of overshoot weightings rather than at a single one, but a designer weighting EMI or peak stress very differently could reach a different conclusion.

VI. Conclusion

What the data supports, and what it does not

For a segmented GaN gate driver in a hard-switched half-bridge, operating-point scheduling of the control word buys at most a few per cent over a single well-chosen fixed word, and the benefit concentrates in one bit — the gate off-bias rail — at high-voltage, light-load, hot operation. Crosstalk safety, the constraint that motivates most of this machinery, turns out to be free once an active Miller clamp is present: the energy-optimal control word is already safe.

This does not show that active gate driving is not worth doing. Table I shows the opposite — the control word spans an order of magnitude in switching energy, and choosing it well matters a great deal. What the data does not support is the further step, common in the literature, from optimised settings help to settings must adapt to the operating point. Those are separate claims, and only the first is well supported here.
