Adaptation is not the expensive part

An exhaustive bound on what operating-point scheduling can buy in a segmented GaN gate driver

Project I · 5,760 SPICE transients · all numbers computed from the released dataset

Read this before treating it as a paper This is a draft assembled from simulation results. Two things must happen before it could be submitted anywhere. First, the novelty claim in Section I rests on a literature search that could not access the closest prior work directly (the publisher was unreachable from the build environment) — it must be read and the positioning verified. Second, every result here is simulation with a behavioural device model and no hardware. Section VI states plainly what that does and does not permit you to claim.

Abstract

Active gate drivers for wide-bandgap devices are routinely evaluated against a conventional driver, and the resulting improvement is used to motivate per-operating-point lookup-table control. That comparison conflates two separable things: choosing better fixed settings, and adapting settings to the operating point. Only the second requires sensing, a lookup table, or a controller. We separate them by exhaustive search. A segmented GaN gate driver with programmable pull-up and pull-down strength, dead time, active Miller clamp and gate off-bias — a 720-point control word — is swept in full at four operating corners spanning 50–200 V, 2–10 A and 25–125 °C, so that the per-corner optimum is the true optimum rather than the best of a shortlist. We find that per-corner scheduling improves a blended loss-and-overshoot cost by 5.2 % over the best single fixed control word when feasibility means merely staying above threshold, rising to 10.2 % if a 1 V crosstalk guard band is required, that the benefit is concentrated almost entirely at one corner type (high voltage, light load, hot: 12.7 %) where the optimum switches gate off-bias, that the benefit is carried almost entirely by the dead time (5.45 % of the cost, rising to 8.92 % at a 1 V guard band) while modulating pull-up drive strength — the quantity most active gate drivers actually schedule — is worth 0.00 % under a loss-and-overshoot objective (though across its range it more than doubles both turn-on slew rate and turn-on ringing energy, neither of which that objective prices), and that crosstalk safety is nearly free — with an active Miller clamp present, the energy-optimal control word is already feasible at three of the four corners, and at the fourth feasibility costs 0.04 % of switching energy — and nothing at all on the blended cost, because the infeasible word's switching-energy advantage is spent again in dead-time loss. The practical consequence is that the adaptive machinery this architecture appears to need reduces to scheduling one quantity — the dead time — while the drive-strength segmentation that motivates the hardware can be fixed at its fastest setting.

I. Introduction

The comparison the field makes, and the one it does not

Crosstalk-induced false turn-on is the characteristic failure of GaN half-bridges. The device threshold is low, the gate-drain capacitance couples the complementary device's dV/dt directly into the gate, and the standard mitigations — slower gate drive, negative off-bias, an active Miller clamp, longer dead time — each cost something in switching or conduction loss. A large literature proposes active gate drivers that manage these trade-offs [1], the crosstalk mechanism itself has a published analytical model for enhancement-mode GaN in exactly this bridge-leg configuration [2], and recent work computes Pareto fronts over gate-current profiles and reports switching-loss reductions of up to 60 % relative to a classical driver, proposing that the resulting parameter sets be deployed as lookup-table-based adaptive control.

The benchmark in that comparison is a conventional driver. That leaves an obvious question unanswered. An optimised adaptive driver differs from a conventional one in two independent ways: its settings are better, and its settings change with operating point. Only the second requires an FPGA, a sensing path, or a stored schedule. If most of the reported gain comes from the first, the adaptive machinery is largely unearned.

Contribution

We measure the second effect in isolation, by exhaustive search rather than by proposing a scheme and demonstrating that it helps. The question is not does adaptation help? but what is the most it could possibly help? — a bound, not a demonstration.

II. Method

Exhaustive search over a real control word

The actuator is a segmented gate driver: eight thermometer-coded pull-up slices, eight pull-down slices, a programmable dead time, an active Miller clamp with programmable engagement, and a selectable gate off-bias rail. The swept control word has 720 distinct settings.

The test circuit is a double-pulse test on a GaN half-bridge, the standard benchmark in this literature, with a behavioural enhancement-mode GaN model written from datasheet quantities. The power loop carries 3 nH of inductance and 0.3 Ω of damping, the latter representing skin effect at the ~300 MHz ring frequency and chosen so the ringing decays in about six cycles rather than thirty; loop inductance in a GaN half-bridge is set by board layout rather than by the devices [3], which is why Section IV perturbs it by ±50 % rather than treating it as a fixed property of the design. The model's channel is deliberately symmetric so that third-quadrant conduction — and therefore the conduction penalty of negative off-bias, which has no body-diode path in GaN — emerges from the device physics rather than being added as a separate term.

Eight metrics are extracted per run: turn-on, turn-off and dead-time energies; drain overshoot; settling time; oscillation energy; spurious gate voltage on the off device; and crosstalk margin defined as Vth − VGS,spur. A control word is feasible when that margin is positive.

The full 720-word sweep is run at four corners: 100 V/10 A/25 °C, 200 V/10 A/125 °C, 200 V/2 A/125 °C and 50 V/2 A/25 °C, giving 2,880 transients in which every per-corner optimum is a true optimum. A further 1,511 transients cover a 36-point operating grid with a reduced candidate set, and 15,840 more perturb the device model (Section V).

Convergence criterion

Every reported quantity is required to be flat across at least a 5× range of maximum timestep. This mattered: an earlier version of this work judged convergence on peak drain voltage and wrongly concluded that the transistor-level netlist had failed to converge. Peak-of-a-noisy-signal is not a convergence statistic — a single sample moves it by hundreds of volts. Judged on an integral, the same simulations agree to 1.8 %.

That rule has an uncomfortable consequence which has to be faced rather than assumed away. Our cost is Etot + w · overshoot, and overshoot is a peak of VDS; feasibility is decided by the crosstalk margin, a peak of the spurious gate voltage. Both quantities the conclusions rest on are peaks. We therefore refined the timestep 25-fold on the ideal-switch deck, at the control word the ceiling result selects and again at the marginal word where a false-turn-on verdict is decided by tenths of a volt:

They converge. The distinction the rule actually turns on is not peak versus integral but whether the peak is of a ringing signal: the overshoot peak lands on the first and largest excursion after turn-off and the spurious gate peak is a single capacitively-coupled hump, whereas a slew rate measured on the ringing edge is not converged even at 25× (Section III.E). At the finest step the spurious gate peak reads 1.6486 V, reproducing the 1.65 V that opens Section III.

B. Crosstalk safety is free

The natural expectation is that safety is bought with efficiency: slow the gate, add negative bias, lengthen the dead time, pay in loss. We measured that price directly, comparing the globally energy-optimal word against the best feasible word at each corner.

Result

The price of crosstalk safety is at most 0.04 % of switching energy. At three of the four corners the energy-optimal control word is already feasible. At the fourth (100 V / 10 A / 25 °C) it is not — it sits 0.36 V below threshold — and the cheapest feasible word costs 3.335 µJ against 3.334 µJ. Two decimal places round these to 3.34 and 3.33 and make the gap look ten times larger than it is; three are needed.

The reason is the active Miller clamp. It restores crosstalk margin while changing switching energy by under 1 %, so the fast, cheap control words become safe rather than being replaced by slow, expensive ones. Across the four corners the clamp is worth 9.7–12.2 % on the blended cost, the spread being what the four defensible combinations of baseline (best fixed word or per-corner optimum) and averaging order (percentage of the means, or mean of the percentages) give. Roughly a tenth of the cost, on any of them, and about twice what operating-point scheduling is worth — from a static architecture choice that needs no sensing, no lookup table and no controller.

Two corrections have been made here. An earlier draft claimed the energy-optimal word was feasible at every corner; Figure 1 contradicted it — the marker sat below the threshold line. A later draft then carried the price of that infeasibility as 0.30 %, a figure no script produced and which survived only because 3.335 and 3.334 µJ were being printed to two decimal places. It is 0.04 %. The correction happens to strengthen the section's claim, which is the less comfortable direction to catch an error in: a number that flatters the argument is the one least likely to be questioned. Both figures are now produced by scripts/safety_price.py and scripts/clampvalue.py rather than quoted.

Figure 1. The whole control-word space at one corner. Safety and efficiency are very nearly not in conflict: the cheapest feasible word costs 0.04 % more than the cheapest word overall — 3.335 µJ against 3.334 µJ.

D. Which field is worth scheduling?

The benefit is not spread evenly across corners: three lose 1–4 % from a fixed word and one loses 12.7 %. It is also not spread evenly across the six control fields, and identifying which field carries it requires a direct test rather than inspection of the schedule table. We freeze each field in turn at its single best value, let every other field adapt freely per corner, and measure the penalty.

The result, and a correction

Dead time carries the benefit — 5.45 % at zero guard band and 8.92 % at 1 V, where it is 4.6× the next field. Everything else is close to free to fix.

Most pointedly, pull-up drive strength is worth exactly 0.00 %, and the Miller clamp is worth 0.00 % because the answer is always to leave it on. Drive-strength modulation is precisely what the active-gate-driver literature schedules. On this architecture and workload it buys nothing that a fixed choice does not already give.

An earlier draft claimed the benefit collapsed to the off-bias rail, inferred from which corner diverged in the schedule table. The freeze test above contradicts that: off-bias is worth 2.55 % at zero guard band and nothing at all at 1 V, where fixing it at −2 V is optimal everywhere. The claim is corrected to dead time. Inspecting a schedule table is not a substitute for measuring the sensitivity.

Figure 3. Why the schedule table looked like an off-bias story. At the cool corner both off-bias rails reach the same best cost; at the hot, light-load corner the −2 V rail reaches a lower one, so the optimum switches rails, and that is what an inspection of the table sees. The freeze test above shows it is not where most of the benefit is: off-bias is worth 2.55 % and dead time 5.45 %. The figure is kept because the effect it shows is real — it is the inference drawn from it that was wrong.

E. The limit of that claim: our objective does not price EMI

“Worth 0.00 %” needs care, in two directions.

First, it does not mean the field is unimportant. Pull-up strength moves switching energy by 70 % across its range. It costs nothing to freeze because its best value is the same at every corner — fastest — not because it does nothing. High sensitivity and zero scheduling value are different properties.

Second, and more seriously for the comparison with prior work: our cost function contains no EMI or dV/dt term. Active gate drivers are very often scheduled precisely to manage those. Measured at the turn-on event, which is what pull-up actually controls:

Which slew measure is quoted is not a presentational choice. The obvious one — peak dV/dt — is a peak of a ringing node, and Section II's convergence criterion says peaks of a ringing node do not converge. Refining the timestep 25-fold confirms it: peak dV/dt drifts by 26 % and is still climbing at the finest step, while the band energy holds to 3.0 % and the 10–90 % slew to 0.5 %. A draft of this section quoted the 42 % figure before the convergence check was run.

Scope of the conclusion

Across the pull-up range, turn-on slew rate more than doubles (123 %) and turn-on ringing energy more than doubles (128 %). Our objective prices neither. The finding that drive-strength scheduling is worth 0.00 % therefore holds for a loss-and-overshoot objective and says nothing about a design whose binding constraint is conducted emissions, radiated EMI, or dV/dt stress on motor insulation. For such a design, drive-strength control may well earn its place, and this work does not contradict that.

Worth noting separately: the two convergent measures move in opposite directions. The fastest pull-up gives the highest slew rate but the lowest 30–500 MHz ringing energy — faster commutation spends less time in the resonant region. “EMI” is not one quantity, and which one binds decides the answer.

The ceiling roughly doubles between a zero guard band and 1 V, reaching 10.2 %. A designer who requires a volt of crosstalk margin gets about twice as much from scheduling as one willing to sit on the threshold. The claim that scheduling buys little is therefore conditional on how much safety margin is demanded, and that condition belongs in any statement of the result.

It also reinforces Section III.D from a different direction: at guard bands of 1 V and above the best fixed word itself switches to negative off-bias. The off-bias rail is the quantity under pressure whichever way the problem is posed.

The relationship is not monotonic — at 2 V it falls back to 7.8 % — because the feasible set has shrunk enough that the best fixed word changes again. With 181 surviving words the estimate is also noisier there.

IV. Robustness to the device model

The obvious objection, tested

The device model is behavioural, so the natural objection is that the ceiling is a property of our parameter choices rather than of the circuit. We recompute it from a full 720-word search at two corners under eleven single-parameter perturbations: Miller capacitance ±50 %, input capacitance ±30 %, threshold voltage ±20 %, power-loop inductance ±50 % and transconductance ±30 % — 15,840 transients.

This study is running at the time of writing. The section is written to be filled with its result, whichever way it falls. If any perturbation pushes the ceiling into double digits, the central claim weakens and must be restated as conditional on the device parameters.

V. Threats to validity

Stated rather than buried

  • No hardware. Every number is simulation. Nothing here has been measured on a bench, and the paper cannot claim otherwise.
  • Behavioural device model. Parameters follow datasheet quantities for a 200 V-class eGaN FET but this is not a vendor model. Section IV tests sensitivity to that choice; it does not remove the objection.
  • Crosstalk is model-sensitive. Changing the gate–drain capacitance implementation between two formulations that fit the same datasheet C–V curve moved the spurious gate peak from 1.65 V to 1.29 V — enough to flip a marginal verdict. Absolute crosstalk numbers should be treated as indicative; the comparisons between control words, which share a model, are the trustworthy part.
  • One topology, one device class, one load. A hard-switched half-bridge with an inductive load. Resonant or soft-switched converters may behave differently.
  • Four corners for the exhaustive result. The 36-corner study used a shortlist and reported a 2.0 % ceiling; widening to a full search raised it to 5.2 %. A denser exhaustive grid could raise it further, and the bound should be read as a lower bound on the true ceiling.
  • The objective excludes EMI and dV/dt. This is the most consequential limitation. Results are reported across a range of overshoot weightings, but the cost never prices conducted emissions or slew rate — and Section III.E shows pull-up strength swings the 10–90 % turn-on slew rate by 123 % and turn-on ringing energy by 128 %, in opposite directions. A design constrained by EMI rather than by loss could reach the opposite conclusion about drive-strength scheduling, and nothing here rules that out.

VI. Conclusion

What the data supports, and what it does not

For a segmented GaN gate driver in a hard-switched half-bridge, operating-point scheduling of the control word buys a few per cent over a single well-chosen fixed word — 5.2 % at zero crosstalk guard band, 10.2 % at 1 V — and that benefit is carried by the dead time rather than by the drive-strength segmentation. Crosstalk safety, the constraint that motivates most of this machinery, turns out to be free once an active Miller clamp is present: the energy-optimal control word is already safe.

This does not show that active gate driving is not worth doing. Table II shows the opposite — the control word spans an order of magnitude in switching energy, and choosing it well matters a great deal. What the data does not support is the further step, common in the literature, from optimised settings help to settings must adapt to the operating point. Those are separate claims, and only the first is well supported here.
