THE THREE THINGS TO KNOW BEFORE THE REVIEW

  1. The problem is real and reproduced. Fastest driver, no clamp: the
     off-side gate hits 1.65 V against a 1.4 V threshold. False turn-on.

  2. The active Miller clamp fixes it, and is worth 10.3% on the cost
     function - about twice what operating-point scheduling is worth.

  3. Operating-point scheduling does NOT pay. The ceiling is 5.2%, measured
     with a full 720-word search at four corners. A single fixed control word
     is nearly as good. This contradicts the original project framing and
     should be reported as a negative result, not hidden.
     See FINDINGS.md sections 13-17.

  And one more that is worth the trouble to explain, because it is the part
  a reviewer will push on:

  4. The benefit is carried by the DEAD TIME (5.45%), not by drive-strength
     modulation, which is worth 0.00% - and drive strength is the thing most
     published active gate drivers actually schedule. But that 0.00% is
     scoped to a loss-and-overshoot objective. Pull-up strength does swing
     turn-on slew rate by 123% and turn-on ringing energy by 128%, in
     opposite directions, and our cost prices neither. FINDINGS.md 24.

WHAT IS AND IS NOT TRUSTWORTHY

     Corrected in FINDINGS.md section 23. An earlier version of this file
     said the SKY130 transistor-level transient "does not converge". That
     was too strong: the convergence test had been run on peak V_DS, which
     one bad sample moves by hundreds of volts.

       Transistor-level E_on / E_off / E_dt   reportable, 1.8% spread
       Transistor-level peak V_DS             NOT reportable
       Low-side hybrid peak V_DS              reportable, 0.04%
       Everything from the ideal-switch deck  reportable, 0.1%

     The rule that came out of it, and which has since caught a second bad
     number: on a ringing node, integrals converge with timestep refinement
     and peaks do not. Do not quote a peak without refining the step first.
